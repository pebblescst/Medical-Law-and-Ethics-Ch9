Chapter focus: Healthcare practitioners have important public health obligations that go beyond caring for individual patients — they are legally required to collect and report data that protects the broader community. Chapter 9 covers vital statistics reporting, the legal framework of public health authority, mandatory disease and injury reporting, and the federal system regulating controlled substances.
Forensic Medicine
Definition: The division of medicine that incorporates law and medicine — involves medical issues or medical proof at trials having to do with malpractice, crimes, and accidents.
Malpractice cases
Forensic experts analyze medical records, examine injuries, and provide expert testimony on whether the standard of care was met or breached — helping courts understand complex medical evidence
Criminal cases
Forensic pathologists determine cause and manner of death; forensic specialists analyze blood spatter, bite marks, wound patterns, toxicology, and other medical evidence relevant to prosecution or defense
Accident cases
Medical evidence establishes the nature and extent of injuries, mechanism of injury, and causation — critical for personal injury litigation, workers' compensation, and insurance claims
Vital Statistics — Definition & Purpose
Definition: Numerical data collected by government officials to assess population trends and needs. "Vital" refers to life events — births, deaths, and changes in a person's civil status.
Four categories of vital statistics collected:
👶
Live births — each birth generates a birth certificate; data tracks birth rates, demographics, and population growth patterns
⚰️
Deaths — each death generates a death certificate; data tracks mortality rates, causes of death, and epidemiological trends
📋
Fetal deaths and induced abortions — tracked separately from live births; provide data on reproductive health outcomes and prenatal care access
💍
Changes in individual's civil status — marriages, divorces, adoptions; affect legal status, insurance, and other population-level data
Who uses vital statistics data?
Educational institutions Governmental agencies Research scientists Private industries Other organizations and individuals
Healthcare practitioners' role: Physicians, nurses, and other practitioners are legally required to complete vital records documents — birth certificates, death certificates, and fetal death reports. Failure to comply is a legal violation, not merely an administrative failure.
Recording Births and Deaths
Guidelines for completing vital records forms:
✓ Type or print all entries — handwriting must be legible; typed entries preferred for legal documents
✓ Leave no entries blank — use "unknown" or "not applicable" where information is genuinely unavailable
✓ Avoid corrections and erasures — corrections create legal questions about document authenticity
✓ Provide signatures where requested — authorized practitioner signature is required for legal validity
✓ File only originals with state registrars — official record requires original document submission
✓ Verify spelling of names — errors in names on vital records cause significant legal problems for families
✓ Avoid abbreviations — use complete, standardized medical terminology
✓ Refer problems to state officials — do not guess; contact the appropriate state registrar for guidance
Death Certificate — Required Information & Autopsy Triggers
Required on death certificate:
Disease, injury, or complication causing death Date and time of death Place of death Pregnancy status (if female) Whether autopsy was performed
Deaths subject to mandatory autopsy:
⚠ Possibly due to criminal causes
⚠ Unattended death — no physician present or no documented medical care
⚠ Cause of death undetermined by the physician
⚠ Violent or otherwise suspicious death
Constitutional Basis & Federalism
Public health authority is inferred from the 10th Amendment of the U.S. Constitution, which reserves all powers not delegated to the federal government to the states or to the people. This is why public health law is primarily state law — each state has broad authority to protect the health, safety, and welfare of its citizens.
Federalism in public health:
Federalism
The sharing of power among national, state, and local governments. In public health, all three levels have roles: the federal government sets national standards, conducts research, and coordinates national responses; state governments have the primary authority to regulate health and safety; local governments implement and enforce state and local health codes.
Six provisions of public health statutes:
→ Guard against unsanitary conditions in public facilities
→ Inspect establishments where food and drink are processed and sold
→ Exterminate pests and vermin that can spread disease
→ Check water quality
→ Set up measures of control for certain diseases
→ Require physicians, school nurses, and other healthcare workers to file certain reports for the protection of citizens
State Enforcement Powers
States have broad police powers to protect the public health — including several powerful enforcement tools that may override individual preferences for the collective good.
Disease outbreak investigations
States can require investigations when infectious disease outbreaks occur — compelling access to premises, records, and individuals to trace and contain the source
Mandatory childhood vaccinations
States can make childhood vaccinations a condition for school entry — the Supreme Court has consistently upheld this authority as a legitimate exercise of state police power. Religious and medical exemptions vary by state.
Tobacco advertising restrictions
Ban the distribution of free cigarette samples around schools or in areas where children congregate; restrict smoking in public places
Smoking bans and restrictions
Enact smoking bans in public spaces — workplaces, restaurants, bars, parks — to protect the public from secondhand smoke exposure
Involuntary detention (quarantine)
States may involuntarily detain (quarantine) individuals who have certain infectious diseases to prevent transmission to the public. This is one of the most powerful — and controversial — public health tools. Must meet constitutional due process requirements.
Property seizure and destruction
States can seize and/or destroy property to contain the threat of toxic substances — contaminated food, hazardous materials, or property presenting public health hazards
CDC — Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
A federal agency providing a variety of protective and safety services and education.

Primary function: Analyzes data to determine solutions for disease threats. Tracks disease patterns, conducts epidemiological investigations, publishes health guidelines, and coordinates national and international public health responses.

Example of services: Notifies the public of food poisoning outbreaks; issues travel health advisories; maintains the national notifiable disease surveillance system; tracks vaccine safety through VAERS.
Reportable Diseases
Reportable diseases are determined by each state — there is no single national list, though the CDC maintains the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS) that states voluntarily participate in. Healthcare practitioners are legally required to report diagnosed conditions from the state's reportable disease list.
STIs — reported when diagnosed
Sexually transmitted infections must be reported to the public health department upon diagnosis — regardless of patient consent. This is a mandatory reporting obligation that overrides normal confidentiality. States typically handle partner notification programs.
HIV notification
Notification of past and present sexual partners or needle-sharing partners of HIV-positive individuals. Many states have partner notification (contact tracing) programs. The tension between patient confidentiality and the duty to warn potential partners is one of the major ethical issues in this area.
Public-facing workers — more stringent reporting
Communicable disease reporting is more stringent for patients who work with the public — healthcare workers, food handlers, teachers, daycare workers — because their infection poses a greater risk of community transmission.
Examples of reportable diseases (commonly tested):
Mumps Pertussis (whooping cough) Poliomyelitis Rabies Tuberculosis (TB) Hepatitis (A, B, C) Salmonella/E. coli COVID-19 Gonorrhea / Chlamydia / Syphilis Meningococcal disease
Vaccines & VAERS
National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act (1986)
Established that manufacturers of certain childhood vaccines would have limited liability for adverse vaccine reactions. Recognized that vaccines carry rare but real risks, and that a federal compensation system was needed to maintain vaccine supply by protecting manufacturers from excessive liability.
National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program (VICP)
A no-fault federal program that compensates individuals who suffer serious adverse reactions to covered vaccines. Patients (or their families) file a claim; if successful, they are compensated without having to sue the manufacturer. Handling the claim first through the VICP is a prerequisite to most civil lawsuits against vaccine manufacturers.
Smallpox Emergency Personnel Protection Act (SEPPA)
Provides compensation for healthcare workers and emergency responders who suffer adverse reactions from smallpox vaccination administered as part of a government emergency preparedness program — separate from the general VICP
COVID-19 vaccines and boosters
Covered under the CARES Act's PREP Act framework during the public health emergency — provided liability immunity for manufacturers and administrators; compensation available through the Countermeasures Injury Compensation Program (CICP) rather than VICP
Required vaccination documentation — 4 elements:
1
Date vaccine was administered — must be recorded accurately; basis for follow-up scheduling
2
Vaccine manufacturer — identifies the specific product given
3
Vaccine lot number — enables tracing in case of recall or adverse event investigation
4
Name, address, and title of healthcare provider who administered the vaccine — identifies the responsible practitioner
VAERS — Vaccine Adverse Events Reporting System:
Healthcare practitioners must report to VAERS:
✦ Any event listed in the Vaccine Injury Table — a published list of adverse reactions that may be compensable under VICP
✦ Any contraindicating event listed in the manufacturer's package insert — events the manufacturer identifies as warning signs or contraindications
VAERS is a passive surveillance system co-managed by the CDC and FDA — anyone can report, but healthcare practitioners have mandatory reporting obligations for covered vaccines.
Reportable Injuries & Abuse
Healthcare practitioners are legally required to report specific types of injuries and suspected abuse to appropriate authorities. These mandatory reporting laws override normal confidentiality protections and exist to protect vulnerable populations.
Reportable injury types:
Assault
Physical attack resulting in injury; must be reported to law enforcement in most states; healthcare providers treating assault victims have mandatory reporting obligations
Rape / sexual assault
Most states require healthcare providers who treat rape victims to report to law enforcement; some states allow victim to opt out; evidence collection (rape kit) must be handled with strict chain-of-custody protocols
Domestic violence — three subcategories
Child abuse: All states require mandatory reporting of suspected child abuse or neglect by healthcare practitioners — no proof required, only reasonable suspicion
Spousal/intimate partner abuse: Reporting requirements vary by state — some require mandatory reporting; others rely on patient consent
Elder abuse: All states require reporting of suspected elder abuse, neglect, or exploitation to adult protective services
Unborn Victims of Violence Act
Federal law (also called "Laci and Conner's Law") that recognizes an unborn child as a separate victim when a federal crime is committed against a pregnant woman and the child is killed or injured. Creates a separate criminal offense for harm to the fetus — important for forensic medicine and injury reporting in cases of violence against pregnant women.
Signs of abuse — Physical indicators:
Unexplained fractures
Repeated injuries
Burns with unusual shapes
Friction burns
Bite marks
Malnutrition or dehydration
Signs of possible sexual abuse
Signs of abuse — Behavioral indicators:
Illogical explanation for injuries — story doesn't match the injury pattern
Frequently changing physicians — "doctor shopping" to avoid pattern recognition
Changes in appetite — may indicate stress, depression, or physical deprivation
Mandatory reporting obligation: Healthcare practitioners who have a reasonable suspicion of child abuse MUST report — even without proof. Good-faith reporters are protected from civil and criminal liability. Failure to report is itself a crime in all states.
Drug Regulators — FDA and DEA
FDA — Food and Drug Administration
A federal agency within the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS). Primary responsibilities in drug regulation:

✦ Tests and approves prescription drugs before they can be marketed and sold in the United States
✦ Evaluates drug safety and efficacy through the New Drug Application (NDA) process
✦ Approves drug labeling and requires accurate package inserts
✦ Monitors post-market drug safety through pharmacovigilance systems
✦ Regulates OTC (over-the-counter) medications, biologics, medical devices, and food safety
✦ Enforces drug advertising standards — pharmaceutical companies cannot make false claims
DEA — Drug Enforcement Administration
A branch (agency) within the U.S. Department of Justice. Primary responsibilities:

✦ Regulates the sale and use of drugs — particularly controlled substances
✦ Enforces the Controlled Substances Act (CSA)
✦ Issues DEA numbers to practitioners authorized to prescribe controlled substances
✦ Investigates drug trafficking and diversion
✦ Maintains the five-schedule system of controlled substance classification
✦ Can revoke prescribing authority and prosecute violations
Key distinction: FDA = HHS, approves/tests drugs before market. DEA = Department of Justice, enforces controlled substance regulations. A practitioner can lose their DEA number (right to prescribe controlled substances) through disciplinary action even without losing their state medical license — and vice versa.
Medical & Recreational Marijuana
Marijuana remains a Schedule I controlled substance under federal law (DEA/CSA) — meaning no currently accepted medical use and high potential for abuse at the federal level. However, state laws have dramatically expanded marijuana access, creating a significant federal-state legal conflict.
Medical marijuana
37 states had laws allowing medical marijuana as of November 2021. Typically requires a physician's recommendation (not a traditional prescription, since Schedule I substances cannot be prescribed). Each state has different qualifying conditions, dispensary regulations, and practitioner obligations.
Recreational marijuana
18 states, two territories, and Washington D.C. allowed recreational marijuana as of November 2021. Adults can purchase and use marijuana without a medical reason. Each state has different laws regarding possession limits, sales regulation, and taxation.
The federal-state conflict: Federal employees (including VA healthcare workers) and federal facilities cannot recommend or prescribe marijuana. Healthcare workers in states with legal marijuana still face risks — federal contracts, DEA registration, and federal financial assistance can all be jeopardized. As a healthcare practitioner, always understand both the state law AND any applicable federal law in your jurisdiction.
Controlled Substances Act (CSA) — Five Schedules
The Controlled Substances Act provides five categories (schedules) of controlled drugs based on their potential for abuse, accepted medical use, and safety or dependence liability. The DEA enforces the schedules. Healthcare practitioners must understand this system for legal prescribing, storage, documentation, and dispensing of controlled substances.
C-I
Schedule I — No accepted medical use; highest abuse potential
Criteria: High potential for abuse + NO currently accepted medical use in the United States + lack of accepted safety for use under medical supervision. Cannot be prescribed — may only be used in DEA-approved research.
Examples: Heroin, LSD, peyote, psilocybin mushrooms, MDMA (Ecstasy), marijuana (federal classification — though this conflicts with many state laws). Note: Marijuana's Schedule I status is the basis of the federal-state marijuana law conflict.
C-II
Schedule II — High potential for abuse; accepted medical use
Criteria: High potential for abuse that may lead to severe psychological or physical dependence + currently accepted medical use (with severe restrictions).
Examples: Opioids: morphine, oxycodone (OxyContin), fentanyl, hydromorphone (Dilaudid), methadone, meperidine (Demerol). Stimulants: cocaine (for topical anesthesia), methamphetamine, amphetamine (Adderall), methylphenidate (Ritalin). Also: PCP.
Prescribing rules: Written or electronic prescription required — no oral prescriptions except in emergencies (and must be followed by written Rx within 7 days). No refills permitted. Strict dispensing records required.
C-III
Schedule III — Moderate to low dependence potential
Criteria: Moderate to low potential for physical and psychological dependence + currently accepted medical use. Lower abuse potential than Schedule I or II.
Examples: Anabolic steroids, codeine combinations (Tylenol with Codeine #3/#4), buprenorphine (Subutex — used for opioid use disorder treatment), testosterone, ketamine, dronabinol (synthetic THC).
Prescribing rules: Prescription may be oral or written; up to 5 refills in 6 months permitted.
C-IV
Schedule IV — Low abuse potential; low risk of dependence
Criteria: Low potential for abuse relative to Schedule III + currently accepted medical use + limited physical or psychological dependence.
Examples: Benzodiazepines: alprazolam (Xanax), diazepam (Valium), clonazepam (Klonopin), lorazepam (Ativan). Sleep aids: zolpidem (Ambien), eszopiclone (Lunesta). Other: tramadol, carisoprodol (Soma), phentermine (weight loss).
Prescribing rules: Same as Schedule III — up to 5 refills in 6 months; oral prescriptions acceptable.
C-V
Schedule V — Lowest controlled substance abuse potential
Criteria: Lower potential for abuse than Schedule IV + currently accepted medical use + limited physical or psychological dependence compared to Schedule IV.
Examples: Cough preparations containing less than 200 mg of codeine per 100 mL (Robitussin AC); pregabalin (Lyrica — for nerve pain and seizures); lacosamide (Vimpat — anti-seizure); some antidiarrheal preparations containing small amounts of opium (paregoric).
Prescribing rules: May be dispensed without a prescription in some states (with pharmacist oversight); requires purchaser to be 18+ and sign a log; limited quantity per purchase.
Memory aid for the schedules: Think decreasing restrictiveness from I to V. Schedule I = most restricted (no Rx possible). Schedule V = least restricted (some available OTC). The lower the Roman numeral, the higher the abuse potential and the stricter the prescribing rules. All controlled substances require a DEA-registered prescriber and careful documentation.
